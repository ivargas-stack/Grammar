Task I: Past Simple Passive & Past Perfect Passive

Passive voice is used when the focus is on the action or the object receiving the action, rather than who performed it.

Formulas:

  • Past Simple Passive: Subject + was / were + Past Participle (V3)
  • Past Perfect Passive: Subject + had been + Past Participle (V3)

When to use which?

Tense Usage New Concept Example
Past Simple Passive A completed action at a specific time in the past. The lost keys were found under the couch yesterday.
Past Perfect Passive An action completed before another action or time in the past (often with words like before, by the time, after, once). By the time the guests arrived, all the food had been prepared.

Key Indicators:

  • Look for time signals like before, by the end of, after, once. These usually require Past Perfect Passive for the earlier event.
  • Simple past time expressions like yesterday, last week, when I arrived often trigger Past Simple Passive.

Task II: Phrasal Verbs

A phrasal verb combines a main verb with a preposition or adverb to create a meaning different from the original word.

Phrasal Verb Meaning New Concept Example
Look away To turn your gaze in another direction. She looked away when the frightening scene started in the movie.
Let out To allow a person or animal to leave / release. The teacher opened the class door and let the students out for recess.
Wear out To use something until it becomes damaged or unusable. After months of mountain hiking, my sports boots completely wore out.
Turn out To end up or result in a specific way. We expected rain during the picnic, but it turned out to be a sunlit day.
Go away To leave a place or go on a trip. Our neighbors have gone away to Canada for the summer break.

Task III: Dependent Prepositions

Certain nouns, adjectives, and verbs are paired naturally with specific prepositions.

Key Combinations to Remember:

  • Fondness + FOR: Expresses a strong liking or affection for something.
  • Story + OF: Indicates the subject or narrative content.
  • Aimed + AT: Directed toward a specific audience or target.
  • Familiar + TO: Well-known or recognized by someone.
  • Doubts + ABOUT: Feeling uncertain or having reservations regarding something.

New Context Examples:

  • He has always shown a distinct fondness for acoustic guitar music.
  • The documentary recounts the incredible story of two mountain climbers.
  • The primary campaign is directly aimed at university graduates.
  • The melody of this traditional folk song sounds very familiar to the local elders.
  • The chief engineer expressed serious doubts about the durability of the bridge.

Task IV: Exclamations with "What" & "How"

Exclamations express strong emotion or emphasis. Pay careful attention to the structure!

Structure Rules:

1. Using "WHAT": Used before a noun (with or without an adjective).

  • What + a/an + (adjective) + Singular Countable Noun (+ Subject + Verb)!
  • What + (adjective) + Plural / Uncountable Noun (+ Subject + Verb)!

2. Using "HOW": Used directly before an adjective or adverb without a noun following it directly.

  • How + Adjective/Adverb + Subject + Verb!

Transformation Examples:

Original Sentence Exclamation Type Exclamation Sentence
This is an extraordinary garden. WHAT What an extraordinary garden this is!
These are incredible paintings. WHAT What incredible paintings these are!
That dog is so energetic. HOW How energetic that dog is!
The scenery looks so peaceful today. HOW How peaceful the scenery looks today!

Task V: Commas in Clauses & Introductory Phrases

In literary texts and poetry, commas create necessary pauses and set off dependent clauses or introductory phrases from main clauses.

Key Comma Rules:

  1. After introductory clauses/phrases: Place a comma after a dependent clause or participle phrase that comes before the main sentence.
    Example: "When reading late at night, I always keep a lamp on."
  2. Prepositional time phrases: Separate time/condition clauses.
    Example: "When after a long journey, we finally found rest."

Demonstration Sample:

Without Commas:
"What a delight it is when reading books I discover a truth that I never knew."

With Commas:
"What a delight it is
When reading books,
I discover a truth
That yesterday, I never knew."
